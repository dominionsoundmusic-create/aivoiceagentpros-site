The problem it solves is missed calls, not staffing

Most small businesses do not lose calls because nobody is employed to answer them. They lose calls because the person who would answer is on a roof, under a sink, mid-appointment, or driving. The phone rings, it goes to voicemail, and the caller rings the next business on the list instead.

An AI receptionist answers on the first ring, every time, including at nine on a Sunday night. That is the whole proposition, and it is a real one. What it is not is a replacement for a good front-desk person who knows your customers by name.

What it handles well

  • Answering and qualifying. Who is calling, what they need, whether it is urgent, and whether they are in your service area.
  • Booking onto a real calendar. The agent reads live availability and writes the appointment back, so the slot is actually held rather than handed to someone to key in later.
  • Consistency. It asks the same qualifying questions on the hundredth call as the first, which is more than most humans manage on a Friday afternoon.
  • After hours. This is where it earns its keep. Calls that would have hit voicemail get captured with a name, a number and a reason.

What it gets wrong

This is the section most vendors leave out, so here it is plainly.

It answers robocalls too

Every automated call that reaches your line gets answered, transcribed, and turned into a contact record in your CRM. On our own number this ran to roughly a dozen a day for weeks — the same recorded message about a Google Business listing, from a different spoofed number every time. None of them were customers. All of them created contacts.

If you buy an AI receptionist and nobody warns you about this, your contact list quietly fills with junk that looks like leads. The fix is a filter that tags short calls automatically, and it should be set up on day one rather than discovered three months later.

Callers can usually tell

The voices are good. The pacing still is not. Most callers work it out inside two sentences, and a caller who feels they were tricked is worse than one who was told. We recommend the agent says what it is.

It does not know what it was not told

An AI receptionist answers from a knowledge base you write. If your prices changed in March and the knowledge base still says February, it will confidently quote February. This is the most common failure we see, and it is not a technology problem — it is a maintenance problem.

It handles the unusual call badly

Angry customers, complicated multi-part questions, anything where the right answer is judgement rather than information. A good setup hands those to a human quickly. A bad one keeps trying.

Who should not buy one

  • Businesses that get very few calls. If you get three calls a week, a monthly subscription is not the answer — answering your phone is.
  • Businesses whose calls are all complex. If most callers need real advice rather than booking or triage, the agent will hand off so often that it adds a step instead of removing one.
  • Anyone who will not maintain the knowledge base. An unmaintained agent gives wrong answers at scale, which is worse than voicemail.

Judge it against what a missed call is worth to you, not against a receptionist's salary. For a trade where one job is worth several hundred dollars, a single recovered call pays for the month. For a business where a call is worth twenty dollars, the arithmetic is different and you should do it before signing anything.

How to test one before you commit

  • Call it yourself, twice. Once as an easy customer, once as an awkward one. Interrupt it. Change your mind mid-sentence.
  • Ask it something it should not know and see whether it guesses or says it will have someone call you back. Guessing is the failure mode that costs you customers.
  • Check what lands in the CRM after the call. The recording is not the point — the usable record is.
  • Give it a week and read the transcripts. You will learn more about what your customers actually ask than from any survey.
